Research

Physical Peers: A Satellite-Based Measure of Firm Similarity

Working Paper

Constructs a novel pairwise measure of firm physical-environment similarity from satellite-derived embeddings of corporate facilities for 2,482 U.S. public firms (2017–2024).

Abstract

Using satellite-derived embeddings of corporate facilities, we construct a pairwise measure of firm physical-environment similarity for 2,482 U.S. public firms from 2017 to 2024. The measure compares firms by the physical character of the places their operations occupy. Industry and product markets explain almost none of its variation. Although the measure reflects geographic proximity, climate and the built environment, more than two fifths survives a specification containing all three. Physical peers are more alike in financial fundamentals and environmental risk exposures, and their returns co-move, even among firms in different industries. Physically similar firms that depend on nature co-experience nature-related incidents, revealing whose nature exposure is realized together. Physically similar firms also cite one another's patents even across unrelated industries and long distances, and the association is concentrated in the citations firms add themselves rather than those inserted by examiners. Satellite-derived measures of firms' physical environments thus capture a distinct dimension of relatedness, along which firms are exposed together and knowledge travels.

Not All Green is Equal: The Financial Payoff of Doing More Good

Working Paper

Distinguishes proactive more good environmental strategies from compliance-driven less harm ones, and shows they carry distinct financial payoffs using LLMs applied to U.S. sustainability reports (2015–2023).

Abstract

We distinguish between two types of environmental activities: "more good" (restoring and enhancing ecosystems) and "less harm" (reducing negative externalities). Using large-language models applied to U.S. corporate sustainability reports (2015–2023), we find that more good actions predict higher future firm valuation, driven by stronger sales growth and increased institutional investor demand. This effect strengthened after 2020 and varies across industries based on strategic relevance. In contrast, less harm activities show no systematic relation to valuation, primarily improving operational efficiency. Results hold across multiple robustness tests. Our findings highlight the distinct financial payoffs of proactive versus compliance-driven environmental strategies, with implications for corporate resource allocation and sustainability policy.
